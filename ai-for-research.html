Understanding AI and Mapping Your Research Process

This first page is diagnostic and conceptual. Before we automate anything, we need an honest account of what these systems are, what they can and cannot do, and where — precisely — they touch the work of research. Only then can automation be designed rather than improvised.

The thesis, stated plainly Artificial intelligence should be used under supervision at the level of each small piece of work — not delegated wholesale, without human judgment, to produce finished scholarship. This is not merely a normative caution. It has a technical basis in how these systems generate text, and it has become the de facto position of the scientific publishers whose rules govern our field.

1.1Overview and objectives

The purpose of this session is not to survey a marketplace of products, nor to certify any tool as the “best.” Products change on a timescale of weeks; the reasoning that lets you evaluate them changes far more slowly. Our aim is therefore to build a durable mental model: an understanding of the machinery beneath the interface, a taxonomy of the failure modes that matter for research integrity, a working knowledge of the rules that journals now impose, and — most importantly — a method for locating the specific points in your own research process where a supervised, small-batch use of AI is genuinely warranted.

By the end of this page you should be able to: (i) explain in functional terms what an AI system is and how a large language model produces its output; (ii) name and distinguish the principal risks, with particular attention to fabricated references as the gravest threat to research integrity; (iii) state what the converging 2020s publishing consensus permits and prohibits; and (iv) produce a diagram of your own research process — its loops and its artifacts — that you will bring to the second session as raw material for building a system.

A note on sources

Where this material relies on industry roundups or adoption statistics, treat them critically. Such figures are produced by commercial actors, are rarely representative, and age quickly. We cite them to illustrate direction and scale, not to establish fact, and we lean on peer-reviewed work wherever it exists.

1.2What artificial intelligence is

Start with an authoritative definition rather than an intuition. John McCarthy, who coined the term in 1955, described artificial intelligence as 14 the science and engineering of making intelligent machines. The standard modern textbook — Russell and Norvig’s Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach — sharpens this into the study and construction of rational agents: systems that perceive an environment and act so as to best achieve their goals.15 Notice what both definitions share: they are functional. They define intelligence by what a system does, not by what it is or whether it “understands”.

That functional frame is a deliberate deflation. The word “intelligence” tempts us to ask whether a machine understands, experiences, or is in some sense conscious; for governing its use in scholarship, that question is a distraction. A system is “intelligent” here to the extent that it performs tasks which, done by a person, we would say required intelligence — recognising a pattern, translating a sentence, drafting an argument. Whether anything is happening “on the inside” is irrelevant to how we should supervise it. What matters is the behaviour and its reliability.

This is exactly where machine “intelligence” and human intelligence part company. Psychology does not define human intelligence by a single task. An American Psychological Association task force characterised it as the ability to understand complex ideas, to adapt effectively to the environment, to learn from experience, to reason, and to overcome obstacles by taking thought.16 Howard Gardner goes further: intelligence is not one general capacity but plural — a set of relatively independent intelligences, among them linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, and interpersonal.17 Held against that, today’s AI is narrow and singular: superb at the one kind of task it was trained on, with none of the general, adaptive, experience-grounded understanding the psychologists describe.

Within that functional frame, the terms most often confused with one another stand in a nested relationship. Artificial intelligence is the widest field: any technique by which machines perform cognitive-seeming tasks. Machine learning is a subset of AI in which the system’s behaviour is learned from data rather than explicitly programmed. Deep learning is in turn a subset of machine learning, using many-layered neural networks; it is the approach behind essentially all of today’s generative systems. Running alongside — overlapping but not identical — is data science, the broader practice of extracting knowledge from data. The distinctions matter because they tell you what kind of thing you are dealing with, and therefore what kind of error to expect.

1.3How modern AI works

Here is the one idea that separates modern AI from ordinary software, in plain terms. Ordinary software follows rules a person wrote: if the total is over $100, apply free shipping. A human thought of the rule; the computer just obeys. Modern AI flips this around. Nobody writes the rules. Instead, the system is shown an enormous number of examples and works out the patterns for itself.

An analogy. You could try to teach a child what a cat is by writing a definition — four legs, fur, whiskers — but it would fail, because so would a dog. What actually works is showing them hundreds of cats until they simply get it. Two consequences follow, and they explain almost everything else in this workshop. First, the model is only ever as good as its examples: it copies the patterns in its training data, including the mistakes and the biases. Second, it has learned patterns, not truth — it has no way to check a claim against the world, only against “what usually comes next”. That is why it can produce a fluent, confident sentence that is simply wrong.

Definition · a model, in one line

A machine-learning model is a huge set of adjustable numbers (“parameters”) tuned on examples until it reproduces their patterns. Its skill is bounded by that data and by what it was trained to do — which, for a language model, is to continue text plausibly, not to tell the truth.

1.4Large language models and generative AI

A large language model is a deep neural network trained on an enormous corpus of text to perform one deceptively simple task: given a sequence of words (more precisely, tokens), predict the next one. Generation is this prediction run repeatedly — the model emits a token, appends it to the sequence, and predicts again. Each prediction is a probability distribution over the vocabulary; the fluent paragraphs you read are the accumulated result of thousands of such probabilistic choices. There is no separate step in which the model consults a database of facts or verifies a claim. Coherence is the objective; correspondence to reality is not.

The Transformer and self-attention

What makes modern language models work as well as they do is the Transformer architecture, introduced in 2017.1 Its central mechanism is self-attention: when processing any given token, the model computes weighted relationships to every other token in the context, allowing it to decide which earlier words are relevant to interpreting the current one. Attention lets “it” resolve to the correct noun many sentences back, and lets the model track the thread of an argument across a long passage. This is why these models capture context so much better than their predecessors — and why longer, better-structured prompts produce better output, a point we will exploit directly in the prompt-engineering section of Page 2.

The crucial point · fluency is not truth

Because the objective is next-token plausibility, an LLM is optimised to sound right, not to be right. A confident, well-formed, entirely false sentence is not a malfunction; it is the system operating exactly as designed. Every downstream rule in this workshop follows from this single fact.

A historical correction worth making

Public discussion of these systems is riddled with folklore, and a workshop on research practice should model the habit of checking claims against the record. Three widely muddled facts about the most-discussed developer are worth stating cleanly. OpenAI was founded in 2015, not later, as a research organisation. Elon Musk, an early backer, left its board in 2018; he is frequently but wrongly described as a current director. And Sam Altman is its chief executive. None of these bears on the technical argument, but the reflex of correcting a plausible-sounding error before repeating it is exactly the discipline the rest of this session is about.

1.5The current state of the field

The field is not static, and the direction of travel is itself an argument for supervision. Four movements are worth understanding.

From prediction toward reasoning over structured knowledge

The most consequential technical response to the “fluency is not truth” problem is to stop asking the model to recall facts from its parameters and instead ground it in an external, structured source at the moment of answering. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) does this with passages of text; more recent graph-based approaches — knowledge graphs and GraphRAG — represent knowledge as entities and the relationships between them, so the model reasons over a curated structure rather than over loosely similar text chunks.5 The empirical claim, now reasonably well supported, is that grounding in structured, factual relationships reduces the surface area for hallucination, because the answer is tied to verifiable nodes and edges rather than reconstructed from probabilities.5 Two cautions belong beside the promise: constructing the graph is itself costly and error-prone, and grounding narrows but never eliminates fabrication.

The rise of agentic AI

The second movement is toward agents: systems that do not merely answer a single prompt but plan, call tools, browse, execute code, and take multi-step actions toward a goal with reduced human intervention between steps. Agentic systems are genuinely more capable and genuinely more useful. They are also, for exactly the same reason, riskier: greater autonomy means more consequential actions taken before a human sees them, and errors that compound across a chain rather than surfacing at a single output. The counter-intuitive implication is central to this workshop — the more autonomous the tool, the more deliberately its oversight must be designed. Autonomy does not remove the human from the loop; it raises the stakes of deciding where in the loop the human stands.

Narrow, general, super — and the graded view

Popular commentary sorts AI into “narrow” (good at one thing), “general” (human-level across the board), and “super” (beyond human). The trichotomy is a useful entry point but too coarse to describe what we actually have. Today’s frontier models are still narrow in the strict sense — they do not possess general competence across all cognitive tasks — yet they are “closed-scope” and extraordinarily broad: within the space of language tasks they range widely and unevenly, expert at some, unreliable at others. A more disciplined framing, proposed by researchers at Google DeepMind, replaces the trichotomy with a matrix of performance (depth) against generality (breadth), with a separate axis for autonomy.4 On that scheme, current general-purpose models are classified only as “emerging” AGI — broadly capable but not reliably matching a skilled adult across most tasks — even as narrow systems reach superhuman performance on single problems.4 The value of the graded view is that it forbids the lazy binary “is it AGI yet?” and asks instead how deep, how broad, and how autonomous — the questions that actually bear on whether you should let a system act unsupervised.

An emerging labour market of oversight roles

Finally, the direction of the field is visible in the jobs it is creating, and their character reinforces the thesis. The roles now appearing around AI systems — ontologists who design the structured knowledge these systems reason over, prompt engineers who specify tasks precisely, model evaluators and auditors who test and certify behaviour, and AI ethicists who assess consequences and compliance — are overwhelmingly judgment-based oversight roles. They exist because capable automation increases, rather than reduces, the demand for human judgment at the points where the machine’s work meets the world. The market is, in effect, pricing in supervised automation.

1.6Limits and risks

To use these systems responsibly you must be able to name their failure modes precisely. The most important, and the most misunderstood, is hallucination.

Definition · hallucination

Output that is fluent and plausible but not grounded in, or not faithful to, the relevant source or the world — content the model generated rather than retrieved or verified. It is a structural consequence of probabilistic generation, not an occasional bug.

The research literature offers a taxonomy worth internalising. The original distinction, drawn from natural-language generation, separates intrinsic hallucination — output that contradicts a provided source — from extrinsic hallucination — output that cannot be verified from the source, whether or not it happens to be true.2 As models moved to open-ended generation without a single explicit source, a second axis was added: factuality (does the claim match the world?) versus faithfulness (does the output adhere to the instructions and context it was given?).3 A model can be factually correct yet unfaithful to your evidence, or faithful to a misleading source yet factually wrong — which is why “it sounded consistent” is never sufficient assurance.

For the working researcher, three practical types recur. First, the confident false claim — a wrong fact stated with the same assurance as a right one. Second, the misreading of a source you supplied — a summary or extraction that quietly distorts what the document said. Third, and gravest, the fabricated reference: a citation that looks impeccable — real author names, a plausible title, a correctly formatted DOI — but points to a work that does not exist, or to a real work that says something else entirely. Systematic studies have found fabrication rates ranging widely by model and domain: an analysis in Scientific Reports found that roughly 55% of GPT-3.5’s citations and 18% of GPT-4’s were fabricated, with many of the “real” ones still containing substantive errors;9 a medical-content study found 47% fabricated and only 7% both authentic and accurate;10 and even recent models tested in 2025 fabricated on the order of 20% of citations, climbing for less-studied topics.11 The fabricated reference is the gravest research-integrity risk precisely because it attacks the citation apparatus that scholarship uses to hold itself accountable, and because it is designed — by the fluency objective — to survive a casual glance.

The surrounding risks

Beyond hallucination sit a cluster of related hazards. Inaccuracy and false information shade into hallucination but also include ordinary errors and outdated knowledge. Plagiarism and copyright arise because models are trained on copyrighted text and may reproduce it, and because uncredited AI-generated prose raises unresolved questions of attribution and originality. Bias is inherited from training data and can be amplified — a serious matter in social-science and public-policy work, where the analysis of unequal treatment is often the point. And misuse ranges from undisclosed reliance that misrepresents authorship to the industrial generation of low-quality or deceptive text. None of these is a reason to abstain; each is a reason to supervise.

A decision framework: when is it safe to use AI?

Rather than a blanket permission or prohibition, adopt a rule keyed to two questions: how verifiable is the output? and how close is it to the scientific claim? Where output is cheaply verifiable and far from the claim — rephrasing your own sentence, reformatting a table — supervision is light. Where output is hard to verify or sits near the claim — a factual assertion, a citation, an interpretation of data — supervision must be strict, and some uses (inventing sources, generating findings) are simply off-limits.

1.7The rules of the game: journals and research ethics

The individual researcher’s judgment does not operate in a vacuum; it operates inside an institutional rulebook that has taken shape rapidly, and unevenly, across the 2020s. When generative tools became widely available at the end of 2022, editors and publishers responded with a scatter of policies. What looked at first like fragmentation has, however, converged on a small set of principles that are now close to universal.

Four commitments recur across the major bodies. First, no AI authorship: tools cannot be listed as authors because they cannot take responsibility for the work — the position taken by the Committee on Publication Ethics6 and echoed by the ICMJE, WAME, and the major publishers.78 Second, mandatory disclosure: any substantive use of AI in drafting, analysis, translation, or image generation must be declared, typically in the methods or acknowledgements.7 Third, human accountability: the authors remain fully responsible for every claim, reference, and interpretation, and must verify AI-produced content.7 Fourth, confidentiality in peer review: reviewers are barred from uploading manuscripts into external AI systems, because doing so breaches the confidentiality of unpublished work.13

Notice what the publishers have actually mandated: that a human must own, disclose, and verify every AI contribution. That is supervised automation, written into the rulebook. The publishing world’s convergence is independent evidence for our thesis — the field’s own gatekeepers have concluded that unsupervised delegation is incompatible with the responsibilities of authorship.

1.9Mapping your research process

The remainder of this page turns from AI to you. Automation is only as good as the map of the terrain to which it is applied — so it helps first to see the whole research lifecycle at once and to mark, honestly, where automation is safe, where it must be supervised, and where it should not intrude at all.

That colouring is the argument of this workshop in miniature, and it is the terrain onto which you will now map your own project. We do so through two complementary lenses, each ending in a short activity; together they produce the diagram you will bring to the next session.

Lens one — process and loops

Research is not a pipeline that runs once from question to paper. It is a system of loops: you draft, you read, you realise the framing is wrong, you revise the question, you re-read, you re-analyse. Redundancy — doing the same kind of thing many times over — is not a defect to be engineered away; it is an intrinsic property of inquiry, the mechanism by which understanding is refined. The relevant economic fact is that the cost of research is per-iteration: what hurts is not that you loop but how expensive each pass through the loop is. This is exactly where AI earns its place. Used under supervision, it lowers the marginal cost of an iteration — a faster first draft to react to, a quicker restructuring to evaluate — so that you can afford more loops, and better ones. Used without supervision, it does the opposite: it lets a bad iteration masquerade as a finished one.

Activity 1 · draw your loops

Sketch your current project as loops rather than a straight line. Mark the two or three loops you traverse most often and, beside each, note what makes a single pass expensive right now. Those expensive passes are your candidate automation points.

Lens two — artifacts

Here is a fact with large practical consequences: AI operates on artifacts. It does not act on your intentions, your half-formed sense of the argument, or the conversation you had with a colleague. It acts on the text, the notes, the data file, the reference entry — the concrete, inspectable objects you hand it. Two things follow. First, the quality of what AI can do for you is bounded by the quality of your artifacts: a tidy, well-structured note produces a useful summary; a chaotic one produces a chaotic summary rendered in confident prose. Second, because the artifact is the unit AI touches, keeping your artifacts tidy and canonical is not housekeeping but infrastructure. This lens is the bridge to Page 2’s central principle.

Activity 2 · inventory your artifacts

List the artifacts your research actually runs on: reading notes, a reference library, data files, draft sections, interview transcripts. For each, mark whether it currently has one canonical home or is scattered across copies. The scattered ones are where a system will pay off first. Bring both sketches — your loops and your artifact inventory — to Page 2.

The Research Workflow: From a Wide Net to a Written Argument

Page 1 was diagnostic. This page is procedural: one concrete, end-to-end way to move from a first idea to a written argument, with AI used under supervision at each small step. The spine of it is a single funnel — cast wide, find the gap, go deep — and every section is one stage of that picture.

2.1The research workflow, on one view

In §1.9 you mapped the research process as nine stages, from finding literature to backing up your files, and marked where automation is safe. This page is where you build a way to move through them. The shape of that movement is a funnel: you cast a wide net across the literature, read it shallowly to find your gap, then read a few papers deeply to build your argument. The tools narrow as you go, and the scholarship — analysing and arguing — sits protected in the middle, exactly as the colours in Figure 1.9 showed.

This diagram is the spine of the page. Every section that follows walks one stage of it, in order — from casting the net, through prompting an AI to extract from what you find, building the triage table, finding the gap, and reading deeply, to drafting. If you lose your place, come back here and ask: am I still casting wide, or have I started going deep?

We move from mapping to building. The session opens with participants presenting the maps they produced in the Page 1 activities — their loops and their artifact inventory. This is not a formality: the system you build should be shaped by your expensive iterations, not by a generic template. By the end you should be able to describe your research as a hub-and-spoke system with a defined data-ownership boundary; specify, per component, what may be automated and how strictly it must be reviewed; formulate prompts as a form of input-side supervision; and articulate an honest, staged adoption plan that leaves your research intact even if any single service disappears.

2.2The two core principles that fuse into one

Two principles govern everything that follows. Stated separately they sound like ordinary good advice; the important claim is that they are the same claim seen from two sides.

The first is the single source of truth. Each piece of information — a definition, a data table, a finding, a reference — has exactly one canonical home. Everywhere else that the information appears is a regenerated view of that home, never an independently edited copy. If your methods section, your slide deck, and your thesis chapter all describe the same procedure, they do not contain three hand-maintained descriptions that drift apart; they contain three views generated from one source. The moment you find yourself copy-pasting a fact and editing the copy, the single source of truth has been broken, and the drift has begun.

The second is supervision per small batch. AI is never turned loose to remake a whole document. It edits the source in small, bounded changes, and the human reviews each change as a diff — a precise before-and-after of exactly what was altered — before accepting it. Small batches keep each review within the reach of human attention; the diff makes the review a matter of reading what changed rather than re-reading everything.

Now the fusion. What does it take for a change to a single source to propagate correctly to every view? It takes a structure in which views are derived from the source by a repeatable process. And what does it take for AI’s work to be reviewable as a diff? It takes exactly the same thing: a single source against which a change is a well-defined difference. The structure that makes updates propagate automatically is the structure that makes AI’s work reviewable. Single source of truth and supervised small-batch editing are not two practices you adopt; they are one architecture, described once by what it automates and once by what it lets you check.

2.3Cast wide, then organise

The first two steps happen entirely outside Obsidian. Run your keywords through Publish or Perish, which queries scholarly databases and returns a large batch — often around a hundred papers, fewer in a settled field, more in a novel one — and export the whole set. Then import all of it into Zotero. Now every paper has clean metadata and a citekey, the short handle you will use to point at it from here to the final bibliography.

What Zotero is for, and what it is not

Zotero and the AI do different jobs. Zotero manages that a paper exists and how to cite it: it stores the PDFs, gives each a citekey, and later generates your in-text citations and bibliography. The AI, in the next step, reads what a paper says. You keep both; neither replaces the other.

2.4Prompting an AI to extract from a paper

It is tempting to treat prompting as a bag of tricks — incantations that unlock better output. That framing is both wrong and unhelpful. Prompt engineering is better understood as a methodological skill: the disciplined communication of your intent, the relevant context, and the operative constraints to a system that works probabilistically and does not understand intent. The model cannot read your mind, cannot infer the unstated, and does not know which of the many plausible continuations you actually want. A prompt is how you tell it.

How prompting connects to the thesis

A good prompt is supervision at the input point. By specifying role, context, task, format, and constraints, you narrow the space of outputs the model will consider — and a narrower output space is a more verifiable one. Where Page 1’s review happens after generation (checking the output), prompting exercises control before generation (shaping what can be produced). The two are the same discipline applied at two ends of the same step.

Why formulation matters, given next-token prediction

Recall from Page 1 that the model predicts each token from the preceding context. Your prompt is that context. A vague prompt leaves the distribution over continuations wide and shallow, so the model falls back on the most generic, average-of-the-internet response — and, when under-specified, is more prone to fill gaps with fabrication. A precise prompt concentrates probability on the continuations you actually want, which both improves relevance and, crucially, makes the result easier to check against what you asked for. Formulation is not decoration on top of the model; it is the lever by which the model’s mechanism is steered.

The anatomy of an effective prompt

An effective prompt is assembled from elements, each with a distinct function. They are not a checklist to recite but components whose purpose you should understand well enough to include only those a given task needs.

  • Persona / role. Tells the model which region of its learned distribution to draw on (“a policy analyst reviewing a regulatory impact assessment”), shifting register and default assumptions toward the relevant expertise.
  • Context. Supplies the situation and the material the task operates on — the audience, the purpose, and, where possible, the actual source text. Context is where you anchor the model to your facts rather than its priors.
  • Specific task. States precisely what to do, as a single well-defined action (“identify the three strongest objections,” not “tell me about the objections”). Ambiguity here is the commonest cause of disappointing output.
  • Desired output format. Specifies the shape of the result (a table with named columns, a paragraph of a given length, a list of questions). Format constraints make the output easier to slot into your system and easier to review.
  • Constraints. States the boundaries — length, scope, what to avoid, what must be preserved (“use only the supplied text; if the answer is not present, say so”). Constraints are where much of the supervision lives.
  • Source / examples. Grounds the task in provided material and, where helpful, in worked examples of the desired output. This is the single most powerful element for reducing fabrication.

Grounded techniques

Three techniques rest on well-established findings rather than folklore. Few-shot examples — showing the model two or three instances of the input-to-output mapping you want — reliably improve conformity to a desired pattern, exploiting the model’s capacity to generalise from examples in context.9 Step-by-step reasoning — asking the model to work through intermediate steps before answering — improves performance on tasks that require reasoning, because it allocates the generation to the sub-steps rather than leaping to a conclusion.10 And iterative refinement — treating the first output as a draft to critique and re-prompt rather than a finished product — matches the loop structure of research itself: you are simply running a fast, cheap iteration under supervision.

Research-specific applications — and their hard limit

In social-science and public-policy work, prompting is useful for a bounded set of tasks: framing literature-review questions (turning a vague interest into precise, searchable sub-questions), structuring an argument (proposing an ordering of claims for you to evaluate and rearrange), grammar and clarity checking of your own prose, and drafting interview questions for you to refine against your protocol and ethics approval. In every case the same limit holds, and it is worth stating without hedging: no prompt, however sophisticated, removes your duty to verify, and none shifts authorship away from you. A better prompt narrows the output space; it never converts unverified output into fact, and it never makes the model an author.

Weak prompt → improved prompt

The improvement is easiest to see by comparison. In each pair below, read the weak prompt first and notice what the model is left to guess; then read the improved prompt and notice how each added element removes a degree of freedom.

Common mistakes and misconceptions

  • Treating output as fact. The most damaging error: mistaking fluency for verification. The prompt shapes the output; it does not certify it.
  • Under-specifying and blaming the model. Vague prompts yield generic results; the fix is usually a missing constraint or source, not a “better” model.
  • Believing a magic phrase exists. There is no incantation. Clarity of intent, context, and constraint is the whole of the skill.
  • Assuming a good prompt cancels disclosure. It does not. Sophistication of prompting is irrelevant to the obligation to disclose AI use to a journal.
  • Forgetting the loop. A first output is a draft. Iterating on it under review is the method, not a sign of failure.
Three connections to keep in view

To hallucination: prompts that anchor the model to a supplied source suppress fabrication, because the model is steered to work from your text rather than its priors. To journal disclosure: however sophisticated the prompt, the use must still be disclosed and the output still verified — prompting changes the quality of the draft, not your obligations. To the work system: a prompt is one part of a flow whose output is still reviewed as a diff against a canonical source; it narrows what enters, but the after-the-fact review does not disappear.

Here is that anatomy applied to the task in front of you: pulling structured information out of a paper so it can go into your triage table. Notice the prompt is deliberately a “dumb” extractor — verbatim only, exhaustive, page-numbered, with no summarising — because paraphrase is where an AI quietly invents.

You are a meticulous research assistant.
From the attached article, extract — using ONLY verbatim
excerpts, as exhaustively as possible — and mark each with
its page number:
  • full APA reference
  • research question(s)
  • variables / relationships (dependent & independent)
  • theoretical lens / key concepts
  • method + empirical setting
  • core findings and conclusion
  • [a field-specific question of your own]
Do NOT summarise, synthesise, or paraphrase. Quote only.
If something is absent, write 'not stated'.

Upload one paper’s PDF, paste this, and you get one row of the table that comes next. The same six moves — persona, context, task, format, constraints, source-grounding — are what make it reliable enough to trust after a quick check.

2.5The triage table

This is the scanning layer. Extract the same handful of fields from every paper into one wide, shallow table — one row per paper — so you can read the whole field side by side. It is disposable scratch work, and it lives outside the vault: a local CSV (which keeps it sovereign) or a Google Sheet. A hundred papers would drown Obsidian; only the survivors will enter it.

An interpretive schema, tuned for public-policy and social-science work rather than pure quantitative extraction, might use these columns: reference · research question · variables and relationships (dependent and independent, understood as conceptual as well as measured) · theoretical lens or key concepts · method and empirical setting · core findings · page-anchored quotes · relevance to my angle · gap or limitation noted.

Why verbatim, and why the page number

Paraphrase is where an AI quietly invents. If the cell is a quote with a page number, you can check it in seconds and you never lose the author’s exact wording — which is what you will need when you write. Where a paper does not state something (a research question, say), the honest cell is “not stated”, not a guess.

This is not yet an annotated bibliography

What this step produces is descriptive extraction — often loosely, and wrongly, called an “AI annotated bibliography.” It records what each paper says. The real annotated bibliography — where you evaluate the work — comes later (§2.9). Keep the two apart in your mind.

Many tools can fill the triage table, and the honest position is that none is perfect — they trade control against convenience in different ways. Match the tool to what you actually need at this step, and treat the list below as current at the time of writing, since these products change quickly.

The tool matters less than three invariants. Ground it on the actual PDF (upload the paper; do not let the model work from memory). Demand traceable output — verbatim excerpts marked with page numbers. And verify anything you keep against the source. This is the supervision principle from Page 1, applied at the input.

2.7Finding the gap — the merenung

Now you sit with the table for a day or two and read across the rows. Patterns emerge, and — more importantly — silences: a question no one has asked, a setting no one has studied, an assumption everyone shares. That absence is your gap; ideally it is both ontological and epistemological, though one is usually enough for a year’s work.

This is the human part

There is no shortcut here, and no tool that finds the gap for you. The table makes the silence visible; you still have to see it. This is the contemplative centre of the whole workflow — the reason the wide net was worth casting at all.

2.8The annotated bibliography: from description to evaluation

Here is the distinction that trips people up. The triage extract (§2.5) describes — it records what a paper says. A scholarly annotated bibliography evaluates — it records what you think of the work: its quality, its authority, its relevance, and how it bears on your gap. One is the machine’s output; the other is your judgement, and only the second is scholarship.

A useful shape, following the widely taught UNSW model, is a citation followed by a short annotation that (i) states the work’s purpose and scope, (ii) notes its method and the authority of its authors, (iii) evaluates its usefulness and limitations, and (iv) says how it fits your own project. You write these for the 10–20 papers you actually read deeply, not for the whole hundred. It is both a thinking discipline and, in many programmes, a required deliverable.

In the vault, this writes itself

Once each paper you read deeply has a literature note carrying its citation and your annotation, the annotated bibliography is a generated view of those notes — never a document you maintain by hand. The mechanics live on Page 3.

2.9Reading deeply: from Zotero highlights to notes

The survivors — the 10–20 papers that speak to your gap — now get read properly, and this is where Zotero earns its place a second time. Read the PDF inside Zotero, highlighting and annotating as you go; then extract those annotations into a note. With the Zotero–Obsidian pipeline, your highlighted quotes flow straight into the paper’s Obsidian literature note, already carrying the citekey and page number. You are not retyping quotes — you are harvesting them.

From there the note grows in your own words: your annotation (§2.9), the concepts the paper raises, and — captured the moment they strike you — your own argument notes, kept strictly separate from what the source said. The division of labour stays clean throughout: Zotero owns the PDF and the citation; Obsidian owns your understanding. The full note system is the subject of Page 3.

2.10Drafting is assembling

Because your claims are already written as short, self-contained sentences with their evidence attached, drafting becomes stitching rather than composing. You arrange the pieces, smooth the joins, and the citations are already in place. Where a claim lives in an argument note, you can embed it, so that improving the note improves the draft. The draft is a view of your thinking — safe to cut and rewrite, never itself a source of truth.

2.11Automation points and supervision levels

Not every component of the system warrants the same treatment. The governing principle is proximity to the scientific claim: the closer a piece of work sits to your findings and your argument, the stricter the review, regardless of how convenient automation would be. Formatting a bibliography is far from the claim; asserting what the data show is the claim. Supervision is therefore best pictured not as on/off but as a gradient.

2.12Tools, in depth

With the architecture fixed, tools can be evaluated for the role they play in it rather than for their marketing. The table below treats the principal AI functions with their strengths and — the part usually omitted — their limitations, and derives two operating rules from a single, well-documented weakness.

The citation weakness, quantified

The rate at which these systems produce confident but wrong citations is not marginal. Peer-reviewed audits have reported fabrication of roughly half of GPT-3.5’s references and about 18% of GPT-4’s, with many surviving references still carrying substantive errors;2 newer models tested in 2025 still fabricated on the order of one citation in five, worse for niche topics.3 Fabricated citations frequently carry real author names and well-formed DOIs, which is exactly what lets them pass a glance.

Two non-negotiable rules follow directly. The verification rule: every AI-suggested reference is an unverified lead until you have confirmed it against the actual record — the publisher, a database, the paper itself — and no formatting polish substitutes for that check. The privacy rule: unpublished manuscripts, confidential data, and material under review do not go into external AI services, both because doing so may breach the confidentiality owed to that work8 and because it moves your canonical material outside your data-ownership boundary. Where such work must be processed, it stays inside the boundary — local models or tools you control.

2.13An operational mindset: the trigger map

A system only works if maintaining it is a habit rather than a project. The habit is captured by a set of triggers of the form “after doing X, update Y.” What makes the trigger map coherent — and worth memorising as a single idea — is that every trigger points upstream, to the canonical source. You never patch the output; you update the source and regenerate. From which the only real rule of the whole system can be stated in one line: if an output is wrong, you fix the source.

2.14From multiple studies to publication: one lineage

The architecture pays its largest dividend across the publication lifecycle. A conference paper, a journal article, and a thesis chapter on the same work are commonly maintained as three separate documents — which guarantees that a correction made in one fails to reach the others, and that the three slowly contradict one another. Under a single source of truth they are instead three views of one lineage, each regenerated from the same canonical sources. The conference paper is an early, narrower view; the journal article a fuller one; the thesis chapter the most complete. A fix to a shared finding propagates to all three along the dependency edges, and the labour of “porting” results between documents — much of it the very drudgery that tempts unsupervised delegation — largely disappears.

2.15Staged, honest adoption

An honest account must acknowledge a real learning curve. Moving to a source-of-truth, version-controlled, local-first system is not free: there is an initial cost in setting up tools and, more significantly, in changing habits — particularly for those of us whose instinct is to edit the output directly. Attempting the whole architecture at once is the surest way to abandon it. Adopt it in stages, each of which is useful on its own, so that value arrives before mastery does.

2.16Ethics and compliance in practice

The architecture is not only efficient; it makes compliance with the publishing rules from Page 1 nearly automatic. Because every change to a source lives in version-control history, you possess a natural, timestamped record of who — or what — changed what, and when. That history is an audit trail: it supports honest disclosure of AI involvement not as an act of memory or good faith but as a matter of record, and it lets you answer, precisely, which passages were AI-assisted and how they were reviewed. Supervised, small-batch editing and truthful disclosure are, once again, the same practice viewed twice.

A second practice concerns material that must leave your boundary — a document shared with a co-author on an external platform, a file uploaded to a collaboration service. Treat any such externally shared document as a disposable “guest room”: a regenerated view you are comfortable exposing, never your canonical source. You furnish the guest room from the hub; you do not move the hub into it. If the platform changes its terms, suffers a breach, or simply disappears, you have lost a view, not your work — and you have kept confidential and unpublished material where it belongs.

Disclosure, concretely

Keep a short, standing note of AI use tied to your version history: the function (e.g. language editing, code drafting), the components touched, and the review performed. When a journal asks for a disclosure statement, you are transcribing a record, not reconstructing one — which is exactly the accountability the publishers require.56

2.17A closing test: sovereignty

There is a single question that tests whether you have built a system or merely a habit of using tools. If any one service you rely on disappeared tomorrow — the assistant, the cloud store, the collaboration platform — would your research remain intact? If the honest answer is no, your canonical sources are living inside something you do not control, and the architecture has not yet been built. If the answer is yes — because your sources are local, plain-text where possible, version-controlled, and yours, with every service reduced to a replaceable spoke — then you have both sovereignty over your work and, not by coincidence, a system in which AI can be used exactly as this workshop recommends: under supervision, at the level of each small piece of work.

Where the two pages meet The supervision the thesis demands and the sovereignty this test measures are the same property of one architecture. A system whose canonical sources you own and regenerate from is a system in which AI’s every contribution arrives as a reviewable diff against something you control — which is what it means to use AI under supervision, one small piece at a time.

Second Brain with Obsidian

If you have ever finished reading twenty papers and then, a month later, could not remember which one said the thing you needed — this page is for you. A “second brain” is simply a place where you write things down once, in a way that lets you find and reuse them forever. We build ours in a free program called Obsidian; the confusion nearly always comes from not knowing what it actually is.

The one thing to understand first

Obsidian is not a website, an app you log into, or a cloud service. It is a program that opens an ordinary folder on your own computer — a folder full of plain text files. That folder is called a “vault”. Nothing is stored elsewhere; nothing is hidden. If you deleted Obsidian tomorrow, the folder and every note in it would still be sitting on your computer, openable in Notepad.

3.1What Obsidian actually is

Imagine a folder on your laptop called Research-Vault. Inside it are text files: one for each paper you have read, one for each idea you are developing, one for your draft. That folder — with all its notes — is your vault. Obsidian is the window you open it through. It adds three things a plain folder cannot: it lets notes link to each other, it shows you those connections, and it lets small helpers (“plugins”) build lists and tables from your notes automatically.

That is the whole thing. There is no database, no login, no magic. You are always just editing text files in a folder you own.

3.2A note is just a text file

Each note is written in Markdown — ordinary text with a few light marks that add formatting. You need very little: # at the start of a line makes a heading, - makes a bullet, **words** makes bold. Because it is plain text, the file stays readable forever and in any program.

# Co-creation

Designing services **with** the people who use them,
not just for them.

- Only real if it covers the *hidden* design choices.
- Connects to administrative burden.

3.3The real idea: links, not folders

Here is the shift that makes Obsidian a brain and not just a filing cabinet. In a normal folder system, a note about co-creation sits in one place and a note about a paper in another, and nothing joins them — to see the connection you must remember it. In Obsidian you join two notes by naming one inside the other with double square brackets: type [[Co-creation]] and you have made a link. And the connection runs both ways automatically — the Co-creation note now shows that this paper mentions it (a “backlink”), with no extra effort.

Do this across your notes and, instead of a stack of isolated summaries, you grow a network — a web of ideas that surfaces connections you had forgotten you made. This is what turns an “information hunter”, forever rummaging through papers, into an “information farmer” who harvests links planted months ago.

You still have folders

Links do not replace folders — you keep both. Folders say what kind of note something is; links say how notes relate. Use folders lightly for tidiness and links generously for thinking.

3.4The four kinds of note

You do not need a hundred folders. Almost everything you write is one of four kinds. Keeping them separate — especially keeping your own thinking apart from what others said — is the single most useful habit, so you never present a source’s claim as your own.

3.6Frontmatter: labels on a note

At the very top of a note you can put a small block between two lines of three dashes. This is frontmatter (or “properties”). It holds labels — the year, the status, the type — that a helper can later read to build lists for you. You write it once; you never make the list by hand.

---
type: literature
year: 2025
status: read
concepts: ["[[Co-creation]]"]
---

3.7Linking and embedding — the essential skill

Four moves, all just typing. The first three point at things; the fourth pulls a note’s content in.

  • [[Co-creation]] — link to a note. The target shows a backlink automatically.
  • [[Co-creation#My angle]] — link straight to a heading inside a note.
  • [[Co-creation|co-design]] — an alias: the text reads “co-design” but still links to the note.
  • ![[Co-design as legitimation]] — an embed (note the !): it shows that note’s content inside this one. Edit the original once, and every place it is embedded updates.
Why embedding matters

This is how a draft gets built from pieces. Write each claim once as its own small note, then embed those notes into your draft. Improve a claim in its note, and the draft improves with it — you never copy-paste the same sentence into three places and then have to fix all three.

3.8Tags and to-dos

Two more plain-text habits. A tag is just #word — a light way to group notes or tasks under a theme (for example #co-creation). A to-do is a checkbox line: - [ ] read Haug et al. Give a task a status by adding a tag — #status/todo, #status/doing, #status/done — and a helper gathers all your tasks into one board, sorted by status. Tick the box and it counts as done. No separate app; it is all just text in your notes.

3.9Plugins and “views”

Obsidian on its own is a good note editor. The power comes from a few free community plugins you install once. For research you need three: Dataview (reads your frontmatter and builds lists and tables automatically — your annotated bibliography, reading queue, and task board are all Dataview views), Templater (fills a new note from a template so every source note has the same shape), and Zotero Integration (imports a paper’s details from Zotero in one click).

What a “view” is

A view is a list the computer builds from your notes — never something you type by hand. If a view looks wrong, you do not fix the view; you fix the note it came from, and the view corrects itself. This is the heart of keeping one source of truth: the note is real; the list is just a window onto it.

3.10Where Obsidian fits in the whole workflow

This is the part that surprises people: Obsidian is only the second half of the research workflow. The first half — casting a wide net of about a hundred papers, triaging them in a table, and spotting the gap — happens outside Obsidian, in Publish or Perish, Zotero, and a spreadsheet (this is the workflow on Page 2). Only the 10–20 papers that speak to your gap ever become notes in the vault.

3.11What Obsidian is not

Three honest boundaries

It does not replace Zotero. Zotero stays your reference manager — it stores the PDFs, gives each paper a citekey, and generates your citations and bibliography when you write. Obsidian holds your understanding. Different jobs; keep both.

It is not heavy qualitative-analysis software. If coding twenty-plus interviews is the core of your method, use NVivo or Atlas.ti for the coding and let the quotes you build on graduate into Obsidian. For a few interviews, Obsidian is plenty.

It is not a system to perfect. Resist building an elaborate note-numbering scheme (you may have heard of “Zettelkasten” — you do not need it). Four folders and plain links are enough. If a system makes you tidy more than you think, it is the wrong system.

3.12Getting started in five steps

  1. Download Obsidian (free) from obsidian.md and install it.
  2. Download the shared vault from ugm.id/RVault, unzip it, and in Obsidian choose “Open folder as vault” and point it at that folder.
  3. Enable the three plugins (Settings → Community plugins): Dataview, Templater, Zotero Integration.
  4. Open 00 — START HERE and follow the five-minute tour.
  5. Change one thing — flip a note’s status, or tick a task — and watch a list rebuild itself. That moment is when it clicks.
The sovereignty test

Everything here is plain text on your own disk. If Obsidian, Zotero, or any single service disappeared tomorrow, your notes, data, and drafts would still open in any text editor. That is the whole point of working local-first: the tools are replaceable, but your research — the thing you actually built — is not.

3.13Living with the vault over time

A good vault is lived in, not maintained. Most of its upkeep is simply using it well; the rest is a handful of light habits that keep it from drifting into a tidy graveyard.

  • Add, don’t reorganise. A new note goes in the right folder and you move on. Resist renaming folders and re-filing things — the [[links]], not the folders, carry the meaning.
  • Never hand-edit a generated view. If a list looks wrong, fix the note it came from, not the list.
  • One canonical home per fact. Never copy a fact into a second note; link to the note that owns it. The moment you copy-and-edit, the drift begins.
  • Keep notes atomic. One idea per concept note, one claim per argument note. When a note grows a second idea, split it in two and link them.
  • Only survivors enter. The vault stays small on purpose: the hundred papers live in the triage table outside it, and only the ten or twenty you read deeply become notes. A near-empty vault at the start of a project is correct, not a failure.
  • Grow it by using it. Before you write, open the concept index or walk the graph — that is the maintenance. You harvest what you planted.
Back it up — it is only text, which is the point

Because every note is a plain file, backup is easy and worth doing from day one. Two good options: keep the vault in a synced folder (Dropbox, Drive, OneDrive) so it survives a dead laptop; or put it under Git, which additionally gives you a full version history — and that history doubles as your AI-disclosure audit trail, showing exactly what changed, when, and where a tool was involved.

One vault, or one per project?

One vault can hold several projects at once — separate them with folders or a #project tag in 50 Project — or you can start a fresh copy of this structure for each project. Either works; the structure travels. What you should not do is scatter one project across three half-vaults, which breaks the single home that makes the whole thing work.

And the rule that governs all of the above, one last time: if a system makes you tidy more than you think, it is the wrong system. This structure exists to get out of your way.
